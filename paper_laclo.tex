\begin{abstract}
This paper reports a qualitative exploratory study of how six deaf adolescents, aged
13 to 15, interacted with LIBRAS Academy, a digital interface for music education
mediated by Brazilian Sign Language (LIBRAS). The study was conducted on April 6,
2026, at a specialized institutional site for deaf education and hearing, voice, and
language support. The empirical corpus comprises a semi-structured first-contact testing
protocol, observational annotations from individual sessions, open-ended comments, and
responses to the Brazilian Portuguese adaptation of the User Engagement Scale (UES-Br).
The analysis characterizes interaction patterns, interface barriers, mediation demands,
and the limits of interpreting UES-Br responses in this context. The sessions showed
that participants could complete the core activities and displayed different forms of
engagement, including reflective exploration, efficient task completion, self-correction,
selective persistence, and image-based progression under reading constraints. Barriers
emerged around task onboarding, visual layout, game pace, scroll mechanics, recognition
reliability, and abstract questionnaire vocabulary. Correcting negatively worded UES-Br
items was essential for interpretation, but the questionnaire remained dependent on
mediation and vulnerable to semantic difficulty and possible acquiescence. The paper
contributes empirical evidence about LIBRAS-mediated music learning and a methodological
reflection on using UES-Br with deaf adolescents.
\keywords{Deaf adolescents \and LIBRAS \and music education \and UES-Br \and
inclusive learning technologies \and accessibility evaluation}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

For many deaf students, music education is still organized around sounds that are
expected to be heard, named, reproduced, and evaluated through hearing-centered
pedagogical routines. Yet musical experience can also be approached through visual
patterns, rhythm, gesture, color, memory, embodied movement, and shared symbolic
activity. Digital learning environments can make these forms of access more concrete,
but only when they are designed and evaluated from the communicational repertoires of
deaf learners. The core difficulty is therefore not simply the absence of music software
for deaf students. It is the lack of evidence about how deaf students interpret
instructions, negotiate support, and remain engaged with such interfaces when they
encounter them in educational settings.

This problem has methodological consequences. If accessible educational interfaces are
evaluated mainly through design intentions, technical demonstrations, or generic usability
assumptions, researchers may overestimate their accessibility. The presence of sign
language, visual elements, or gesture recognition does not guarantee that instructions
are clear, that task rhythm fits the user, that visual layout supports interpretation, or
that a post-use questionnaire is linguistically accessible. In this context, engagement is
also difficult to interpret. A participant may complete tasks efficiently without wanting
to continue, persist in one activity while rejecting another, or report positive
impressions while requiring extensive mediation. For inclusive learning technologies,
these differences matter because they affect not only how users respond to the platform,
but also the reliability of the evidence used to improve it.

Relevant prior work addresses parts of this challenge. Benites' research on music
education for deaf children proposed and developed LIBRAS Academy, an interface that
combines musical notes, colors, Brazilian Sign Language (LIBRAS), and
artificial-intelligence-supported gesture interaction~\cite{benites2024,benites2024laclo}.
Research on assistive educational technology grounded in semiotic theory has shown how
images, written language, and LIBRAS signs can be combined in learning software for deaf
and hearing children~\cite{rocha2016,rocha2023uais}. In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI),
the User Engagement Scale (UES) and its Brazilian Portuguese adaptation, UES-Br, provide
a structured way to describe perceived engagement across dimensions such as focused
attention, perceived usability, aesthetic appeal, and
reward~\cite{obrien2010ues,miranda2021uesbr}. These contributions leave open an empirical
question: how do deaf adolescent users interact with a LIBRAS-mediated music learning
interface for the first time, and how should engagement instrument responses be
interpreted when the questionnaire requires translation and adult support?

The research gap addressed in this paper is twofold. Empirically, there is still limited
evidence, especially in the Brazilian and Latin American context, on how deaf students
interact with sign-language-mediated music learning interfaces in first-contact sessions.
Methodologically, little is known about how UES-Br responses should be interpreted when
respondents are deaf adolescents and the questionnaire requires translation, explanation,
or adult mediation. This paper does not claim to measure learning gains, validate UES-Br
for this population, or provide statistical evidence of platform effectiveness. Instead,
it offers an exploratory, qualitative account of interaction and engagement in
first-contact sessions.

The study was conducted on April 6, 2026, at the Children's Hearing Center (CeAC),
within the Division of Education and Rehabilitation of Communication Disorders (DERDIC),
at the Pontifical Catholic University of São Paulo (PUC-SP), in São Paulo, Brazil. Six
deaf adolescents aged 13 to 15 participated in individual first-contact sessions with
LIBRAS Academy, followed by a mediated UES-Br questionnaire. The analysis is guided by
three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item RQ1: How do deaf adolescents aged 13 to 15 interact with LIBRAS Academy in
  individual first-contact sessions?
  \item RQ2: What forms of engagement are suggested by triangulating behavioral
  observation, UES-Br responses, and open-ended feedback?
  \item RQ3: How do mediation conditions and linguistic accessibility affect the
  interpretability of UES-Br responses in this context?
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work on
DHH technologies, LIBRAS-mediated music learning, engagement measurement, and accessible
evaluation methods, building toward the gap the study addresses. Section III describes
the study context, participants, protocol, UES-Br scoring, and qualitative analysis
procedure. Section IV reports the results, beginning with observed interaction patterns
and interface barriers before treating UES-Br as complementary evidence. Section V
discusses the empirical and methodological implications of the findings, including
limitations and directions for future work. Section VI concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

Music education for deaf learners has long been designed around hearing-centered
assumptions, yet deaf individuals can perceive rhythm, vibration, and visual patterns in
music, and digital interfaces can extend those channels of access. Benites addressed this
design challenge through a research program that produced LIBRAS Academy, a music
learning environment in which notes are associated with colors, handshapes, and
gesture-recognition feedback mediated by Brazilian Sign Language
(LIBRAS)~\cite{benites2024,benites2024laclo}. In this approach, musical notes are not
only heard; they are seen, enacted, named, and compared through sign-based interaction,
allowing a learner without residual hearing to navigate the interface through visual and
LIBRAS-mediated cues. Rocha and colleagues developed complementary principles for
LIBRAS-based educational software grounded in semiotic theory~\cite{rocha2016,rocha2023uais},
showing that when images, written language, and signs are combined in educational
software, their value depends on whether learners can connect those resources during
activity, not on whether each is individually present. Applied to LIBRAS Academy, this
principle means that a musical note, a color, a handshape, a written label, and a
feedback message form a network: accessibility depends on the coherence of the whole
arrangement, not only on the presence of LIBRAS content. Neither Benites nor Rocha,
however, evaluated how deaf adolescents actually interact with such an interface under
realistic first-contact conditions -- a gap this study addresses directly.

The broader HCI literature on digital technologies for deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH)
learners reinforces why that gap matters. A recent systematic review of over a hundred
papers on DHH technologies identified language learning, communication support, and
family sign language practice as the dominant research areas; music education and
engagement-based interfaces for deaf learners remain underrepresented~\cite{zhao2025dhhchildren}.
Sign-language applications that do exist show that their value cannot be assumed from
design intentions: Chuan and Guardino~\cite{chuan2016smartsignplay} demonstrated that
even well-designed ASL applications for DHH children and families depend on whether
users can coordinate signs, feedback, visual prompts, and task goals in context -- an
empirical question, not a design guarantee. More fundamentally, Korte et al.
~\cite{korte2014requirements} showed that requirements and usability judgments for deaf
children cannot be inferred from adult or hearing-user expectations: language
development, communication mediation, and ways of expressing difficulty all shape how
children interpret and respond to a system. These findings generalize to post-use
evaluation: instruments and protocols designed for hearing adults may produce unreliable
or uninterpretable responses with DHH populations when applied without adaptation.

Measuring engagement provides a structured way to complement behavioral observation, but
raises its own challenges in this context. The User Engagement Scale (UES), developed by
O'Brien and Toms~\cite{obrien2010ues}, operationalizes engagement as a multidimensional
construct covering Focused Attention, Perceived Usability, Aesthetic Appeal, and Reward.
These dimensions capture aspects of perceived experience that behavioral observation
alone cannot: whether a user felt in control, found the interface visually appealing, or
considered the activity worthwhile. The UES-Br adaptation by Miranda, Li, and
Darin~\cite{miranda2021uesbr} provides a validated Portuguese-language version for
Brazilian HCI contexts, and was selected here because using an English-language
instrument with Brazilian adolescents would introduce an avoidable validity threat.
However, UES-Br was validated with adult Brazilian participants; it has not been applied
with deaf populations of any age, and several of its items -- such as ``I let myself be
carried away'' and ``gratifying'' -- involve idiomatic or abstract vocabulary that may be
difficult regardless of the language of administration. Doherty and Doherty argue that
engagement research should use a diverse methodological toolkit rather than treating any
instrument as a direct window onto the phenomenon itself~\cite{doherty2018engagement}. In
this study, that argument shapes the analysis: UES-Br responses are treated as evidence
of perceived experience, while observational records are treated as evidence of behavior,
and their convergence or divergence is analytically informative precisely because the two
do not measure the same thing.

The methodological challenge of eliciting reliable self-report from deaf adolescents runs
through all of the above. Young learners' judgments of their own experience are shaped by
affect, social context, available vocabulary, and the conditions under which a question
is asked, not only by the interaction itself~\cite{rocha2023metacog,vanloon2021}. For DHH
learners, these conditions are more demanding: spoken think-aloud, self-administered
questionnaires in written form, and post-task interviews all assume communicational
conditions that may not hold for participants responding through sign language, limited
written Portuguese, or adult mediation~\cite{albalhareth2022}. There is no established
protocol for conducting accessible UX evaluation with deaf adolescents in
sign-language-mediated settings. The present study contributes to filling that gap by
combining behavioral observation, a mediated UES-Br administration, and open-ended
comments, and by reporting not only what participants did and said, but the specific
conditions under which each form of evidence was produced.

\section{Methodology}

\textbf{Study design.} This is a qualitative exploratory study that characterizes how six
deaf adolescents interacted with LIBRAS Academy during individual first-contact sessions,
and how their perceived engagement, reported through UES-Br, can be triangulated with
behavioral observation. The design prioritizes rich description of situated interaction
over measurement of outcomes or generalization.

\textbf{Setting.} All sessions were conducted on April 6, 2026, at the Children's
Hearing Center (CeAC), within DERDIC, at PUC-SP, in São Paulo, Brazil. DERDIC is a
specialized educational and clinical center that operates at the intersection of deaf
education, hearing and language rehabilitation, LIBRAS support, and family-facing
services. This setting was chosen because it provides access to deaf adolescents in a
context where sign language use, family mediation, and inclusive education practices are
part of the institutional routine, making it ecologically appropriate for evaluating an
accessible learning interface.

\textbf{Participants.} Six deaf adolescents aged 13 to 15 were recruited through the
institution. Inclusion criteria were: being a student or service user of DERDIC/CeAC,
having at least some familiarity with LIBRAS, and having a responsible adult present
during the session. Participants had not previously used LIBRAS Academy. They are
identified by codes P1 to P6 to preserve anonymity. Table~\ref{tab:participants}
summarizes their profiles; differences in hearing history, LIBRAS background, prior music
experience, and written Portuguese fluency are analytically relevant because they shaped
how each participant interpreted instructions, managed task demands, and responded to the
post-use questionnaire.

\textbf{Session protocol.} Each session was conducted individually and followed a
structured script. The research team comprised three members: one researcher dedicated
to interacting with the participant and guiding the task sequence, one researcher
responsible for observational annotation and audiovisual documentation, and a LIBRAS
interpreter who translated task instructions and questionnaire items when needed. A
responsible adult was present in all sessions. Sessions began with a profile interview
answered by the responsible adult and, when possible, by the participant, covering
hearing history, LIBRAS background, prior music and technology experience. The main task
block comprised seven activities presented in fixed order: free exploration of the home
screen; ``Alphabet in LIBRAS''; ``What Are the Musical Notes?''; ``Conductor of Notes'';
``Musical Rainbow''; ``Note Collector''; and a free-choice final task. Participants could
request help at any moment and could indicate readiness to advance to the next activity.
Total session duration ranged from 7 to 18 minutes of active task time across participants.

\textbf{Data collection.} The empirical corpus comprises: manual observational
annotations recorded in real time by the documentation researcher; task progression notes
indicating which phases were reached in each activity; mediation records noting when and
how support was provided; and post-use responses to UES-Br administered through Google
Forms. The UES-Br was completed by each participant immediately after the tasks, with
verbal item-by-item translation by the LIBRAS interpreter and, when necessary, additional
clarification by the responsible adult. Participants responded at their own pace;
responses were entered into Google Forms by the participant or, when needed, by the
interpreter. Screen recordings were collected as part of the documentation protocol but
are not analyzed here as a coded corpus; observational notes are the primary qualitative
source.

\textbf{UES-Br scoring.} The instrument contains 30 Likert items (scale 1--5) grouped
into four dimensions: Focused Attention (FA, 7 items), Perceived Usability (PU, 8
items), Aesthetic Appeal (AE, 5 items), and Reward (RW, 10 items), plus one open-ended
prompt. FA and AE contain only positively worded items and were computed as item means.
PU contains seven negatively worded items (PU1--PU6 and PU8) and one positively worded
item (PU7); it is reported as both a raw mean and a corrected mean, where negatively
worded items are inverted using $\text{score}_{\text{corr}} = 6 - \text{score}_{\text{raw}}$
before averaging. RW3 is negatively worded and RW is likewise reported in both raw and
corrected form. All individual item responses were verified by manual inspection against
the original PDF exports of the Google Forms records prior to computing means. Given the
small sample and the mediated administration, UES-Br results are used descriptively and
as complementary evidence of perceived experience, not for inferential claims.

\textbf{Qualitative analysis.} Observational annotations were coded using a hybrid
thematic procedure. An initial set of codes was derived deductively from the three
research questions; additional codes were developed inductively through close reading of
the annotation corpus. The coding was performed by one researcher and reviewed in a
collaborative session with the full research team, where codes were consolidated and
discrepancies resolved by consensus. The final codebook comprised five categories:
interface barriers (layout, pace, recognition, scale); persistence and voluntary
continuation; curiosity and exploratory behavior; instrument accessibility problems; and
mediation and social context. Coded events were organized by participant and activity to
support cross-case comparison. The analysis does not reach theoretical saturation; it
identifies recurring patterns and contrastive cases across six first-contact sessions. In
the triangulation, UES-Br responses and observational codes are treated as distinct forms
of evidence -- perceived experience and observed behavior, respectively -- whose
convergence or divergence is analytically informative.

\textbf{Ethical considerations.} The study was conducted in accordance with applicable
ethical guidelines for research involving minors. Informed consent was obtained from
responsible adults; participants provided verbal assent. \textit{[Ethics approval number
to be inserted before submission.]} Participation was voluntary and participants could
withdraw at any time without consequence. Anonymity is preserved through participant
codes P1--P6; no names, school identifiers, or other direct identifiers appear in the
analysis or reporting.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Six Sessions, Six Distinct Trajectories}

The six sessions followed the same activity sequence, but they did not produce a single
engagement profile. Each participant made the interface readable in a different way:
through reflective inquiry, efficient completion, self-monitoring, selective persistence,
image-based navigation, or autonomous interpretation. Duration ranged from 7~minutes and
25~seconds (P4) to 18~minutes and 9~seconds of active task time (P5) --- a range that
does not index more or less engagement: P4, the fastest, persisted in one activity until
a progress bar was full; P5, the longest, showed reading difficulty and possible social
desirability in the questionnaire. Table~\ref{tab:activity-matrix} maps the dominant
form of support each participant required across the seven activities

\textbf{Reflective inquiry.} P1 asked whether the tool was Brazilian or American, spelt
out his own name in the alphabet game, questioned the double meaning of ``Sol'' --- a
musical note and the word for the sun --- and entered the computer's system settings to
comment on its speed relative to his own machine. These are not digressions: they are
evidence that engagement with a sign-language educational tool can extend to its cultural
origin, linguistic assumptions, and technical infrastructure. His session was the second
longest (14:36), not because of difficulty, but because inquiry was part of how he
participated.

\textbf{Efficient completion without voluntary extension.} P2 grasped activity goals
quickly and executed them with little disruption --- recalling Do, Re, Mi before
identifying Fa and Sol, progressing through the alphabet with localized difficulty on
specific letters. Yet efficiency did not carry over into continuation: at the free-choice
stage he did not want to explore further and proceeded only after encouragement. An
interface can be navigable without being the kind of experience a participant would
choose to extend.

\textbf{Self-monitoring and repair.} P3's session was the most consistently autonomous.
She moved through long activity sequences with support only on specific alphabet letters
and showed no structural barriers across the music tasks. The clearest evidence came in
the free-choice stage: during the memory game she made an error, recognized it, and
corrected it without assistance, then continued in ``Build the Word'' with sustained
performance. The ability to notice a mistake, evaluate it, and repair it is qualitatively
different from task execution: it requires that the participant be using the interface's
feedback as genuine information, not merely responding to prompts.

\textbf{Selective persistence.} P4 completed the sequence faster than any other
participant. Her impatience with slower tasks was observable: she did not wait for
``Conductor of Notes'' to finish and encountered difficulty in ``Musical Rainbow'' when
her pace exceeded the interface's feedback rhythm. Yet she remained with ``Note
Collector'' until the progress bar was fully completed. This pairing --- disengagement
from rhythm-dependent tasks, commitment to a progress-based one --- shows that the
appropriate unit for engagement analysis is the task, not the session.

\textbf{Image-based navigation.} P5 oriented strongly toward images and colors rather
than written labels, needed support to activate the camera, and progressed through the
music tasks using color-to-note associations as his primary cue. His session was the
longest (18:09), though the observer explicitly noted a possible desire to please the
researchers --- a contextual factor that bears directly on how his questionnaire
responses should be read.

\textbf{Autonomous interpretation, system-revealed.} P6's stronger written Portuguese
fluency allowed him to read interface instructions without consistent mediation. His
difficulties were localized and produced by the system rather than by his understanding:
a recognition failure for the letter~O (a correct handshape the gesture system did not
register) and a brief hesitation around Sol. When linguistic access to written content is
strong, the system's own recognition limitations become the primary obstacle.

\subsection{Where Access Broke Down}

Most mediation was local: once an activity rule or expected action was clarified,
participants could proceed. The more consequential barriers were those that explanation
alone could not resolve.

\textbf{Visual layout and scale.} P2 had initial difficulty in ``Musical Rainbow''
because notes were distributed across different vertical positions rather than aligned on
a shared line, disrupting the visual mapping between sequence and action. P1 required a
zoom adjustment because the default scale was too small for comfortable use. These
barriers reflect layout decisions that assume a visual context the interface does not
consistently provide.

\textbf{Pace and temporal control.} P2 perceived ``Note Collector'' as too slow and
tried clicking to accelerate it. P4 was asked by the mediator to slow down in
``Conductor of Notes,'' and her haste in ``Musical Rainbow'' interacted with the task's
requirement for deliberate sequencing. Temporal control --- the ability to match or
adjust the rhythm of an activity --- is an accessibility dimension independent of LIBRAS
content presence.

\textbf{Recognition and input reliability.} P5's scroll interaction made the ``see
again'' button in ``Musical Rainbow'' unreachable, interrupting an activity that depends
on replay. P6's correct handshape for the letter~O was not recognized by the gesture
system. In both cases the barrier originated in the system, not in the participant's
competence or effort.

Accessible design for sign-language-mediated music learning therefore requires attention
to visual coherence, temporal flexibility, and recognition reliability --- not only to
whether LIBRAS content is present.

The UES-Br data in Table~\ref{tab:uesbr} did not simply confirm or contradict the
observations. It clarified some cases, complicated others, and exposed limits in the
evaluation procedure itself.

Where the data clarified, it added a self-report dimension that observation alone cannot
provide. P3's fluent, self-correcting interaction is accompanied by the strongest reward
scores in the dataset (RW corr.\thinspace=\thinspace4.00) and consistent aesthetic
appeal --- the questionnaire confirms that what appeared behaviorally as engagement was
also experienced as worthwhile and visually appealing. P6's autonomous, mostly
unmediated interaction corresponds to moderate, coherent scores across all dimensions.
In these two cases, the two sources of evidence tell a consistent story.

Where the data complicated, it revealed that behavioral evidence and self-report can
point in different directions --- and that both directions are real. P4 shows the
sharpest discrepancy: the lowest focused attention (FA\thinspace=\thinspace2.29) and
aesthetic appeal (AE\thinspace=\thinspace1.80) in the dataset, while the observational
record shows she stayed with ``Note Collector'' until the progress bar was full. The
scores describe low absorption; the behavior describes goal-directed commitment to a
specific task. P1 completed the activities with local mediation but required extensive
explanation for questionnaire expressions such as ``I let myself be carried away,''
``aesthetically pleasing,'' and ``gratifying'' --- vocabulary the interface itself never
demanded from him. His scores document a difficult questionnaire administration, not a
difficult interaction. P2 combined efficient completion with limited voluntary
continuation, yet his reward scores are among the higher in the group
(RW corr.\thinspace=\thinspace3.80), and his open response --- ``the experience showed
the importance of trying a game in LIBRAS and created in LIBRAS'' --- is the most
explicit endorsement of the platform across all participants. Each of these signals
captures something the others do not.

Before correction, the raw PU means also complicated interpretation. Most PU items are
negatively worded --- statements about frustration, confusion, or difficulty --- so a low
raw mean indicates less reported friction, not worse usability. P5's raw PU of 1.62
becomes a corrected mean of 4.88 after inversion; P3 and P6 move in the opposite
direction, from raw PU means of 3.62 and 3.38 to corrected means of 2.62 and 2.88.
Reporting raw PU alone would have inverted the interpretation of these three cases.

Where the data exposed limits, it revealed specific conditions under which UES-Br
responses in this context should not be taken at face value. Three threats emerged. The
first is \textit{semantic inaccessibility}: items requiring idiomatic or abstract
expressions --- ``I let myself be carried away,'' ``aesthetically pleasing,''
``gratifying'' --- required negotiation with the interpreter before participants could
respond, so the resulting score reflects that negotiation alongside the original
experience. The second is \textit{social desirability}: P5 produced the highest
corrected PU, AE, and RW in the dataset in a session the observer described as showing
reading difficulty, image-based orientation, and apparent disinterest in some tasks.
Treating those scores as straightforward evidence of high engagement would misrepresent
what happened. The third is \textit{mediation effect}: when an interpreter or
responsible adult reads items aloud and records answers, the relational dynamic changes
the social meaning of agreement. These three threats do not invalidate UES-Br as an
instrument; they define the conditions under which its scores in this study require
triangulation rather than direct interpretation.


\section{Discussion}

The six sessions described in this paper produce three answers that together address the
research questions. In response to RQ1, first-contact engagement with LIBRAS Academy was
not a uniform experience: it took six behaviorally distinct forms --- reflective inquiry,
efficient completion, self-monitoring, selective persistence, image-based navigation, and
autonomous interpretation --- none of which is reducible to the others or to a gradient
from more to less successful. In response to RQ2, triangulating behavioral observation
with UES-Br responses was analytically productive precisely because the two sources
diverged: P4's low attention scores coexisted with goal-directed persistence, P1's
successful task interaction coexisted with questionnaire failure, and P2's efficient
completion coexisted with enthusiastic endorsement of the platform. These divergences
are not noise; they are evidence about how engagement was organized in each case. In
response to RQ3, UES-Br scores in this mediated evaluation context were subject to three
specific threats --- semantic inaccessibility, social desirability, and mediation effect
--- that required triangulation rather than direct interpretation.

These findings connect to and extend what the related literature established. Korte
et al.~\cite{korte2014requirements} showed that usability requirements for deaf children
cannot be inferred from adult or hearing-user expectations; our results extend that
argument to post-use evaluation: engagement instruments, not only interface
requirements, need adaptation for this population. Chuan and
Guardino~\cite{chuan2016smartsignplay} showed that the value of sign-language
applications depends on whether users can coordinate signs, feedback, visual prompts,
and task goals in context. The barriers observed here specify what that coordination
failure looks like: P2's layout barrier disrupted the visual chain between note position
and expected action; P4's pace mismatch disrupted the temporal chain between the
interface's feedback rhythm and the learner's anticipation; P5's scroll barrier removed
the ability to use the interface's replay function for self-regulation; P6's recognition
failure broke the correspondence between a correct gesture and the system's response.
None of these is a localized defect in the ordinary usability sense. Each is a breakdown
in a chain of interdependent resources --- LIBRAS sign, color, musical note name,
gesture, feedback, written label --- where access depends on whether the chain holds.
This shifts the design question from ``is LIBRAS present?'' to ``does the interface make
the relation among its resources legible enough for autonomous action?''

From this perspective, the six engagement trajectories are not six individual differences
in motivation or ability. They reflect six ways of building that legibility under
different communicational and linguistic conditions. P6 could read written labels
directly and so encountered the system's recognition limits as his primary barrier;
P5 could not rely on written labels and so built legibility through color-based
associations, a path that worked until a scroll problem made replay inaccessible.
P3 could monitor her own errors and use feedback to correct them; P2 could complete
tasks without finding them compelling enough to extend. Understanding these differences
matters for inclusive educational technology beyond LIBRAS Academy: it suggests that
accessible design must account for the multiple paths through which different users
construct access to the same interface, not only for the presence of accessibility
features.

The UES-Br findings carry a methodological implication for the field. The three
interpretability threats identified are not specific to this instrument or to this
study: they describe conditions that will arise in any mediated self-report evaluation
with deaf adolescents. Semantic inaccessibility will affect any instrument that uses
idiomatic or abstract vocabulary without LIBRAS-adapted items. Social desirability will
affect any evaluation conducted by researchers in institutional settings where
participants have limited power to express negative assessments. Mediation effect will
affect any protocol in which a third party reads and records the participant's response.
Together, they indicate that accessible UX evaluation with deaf adolescents cannot be
reduced to translating an instrument into Portuguese or providing a LIBRAS interpreter.
It requires rethinking how items are formulated, how the evaluation is socially
positioned, and how responses are recorded --- moving from an instrument administered
after use toward an evaluation arrangement designed for the specific communicational
conditions of the participant.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Three constraints define the scope of the study's claims. The sample comprises six
participants tested in a single first-contact session at one institution, which means
the results cannot be generalized and should be read as exploratory evidence about what
engagement with LIBRAS Academy looked like under these specific conditions. The corpus
relies on manual observational annotations and mediated UES-Br responses rather than a
fully coded audiovisual record, which limits the precision with which behavioral events
can be reconstructed. The evaluation conditions themselves --- researcher presence,
institutional familiarity, and mediated questionnaire administration --- shaped what was
measured alongside what was experienced, which means the study cannot cleanly separate
engagement from the social conditions of its observation.

These constraints point to three directions that follow directly from what was found.
The six engagement trajectories identified here raise the question of which are
systematic and which are idiosyncratic to these six individuals; additional sessions
with a broader and more varied participant set are needed to answer it. The finding that
first-contact performance does not index sustained engagement or learning motivates
longitudinal work: repeated sessions could examine whether participants become more
autonomous over time, whether barrier categories shift as familiarity grows, and whether
LIBRAS-mediated music activities contribute to outcomes beyond music itself. Finally,
the three interpretability threats identified for UES-Br (semantic inaccessibility,
social desirability, mediation effect) and the three interface barrier categories
(visual layout, temporal control, recognition reliability) define a concrete agenda for
the next study: address the instrument before re-administering it, and address the
interface before re-testing it.

\section{Conclusion}

First-contact engagement with a sign-language-mediated music learning interface is not a
single experience. Across six deaf adolescents, it took six behaviorally distinct forms,
none reducible to the others, and none fully captured by a single score or by task
completion alone. That finding has implications beyond LIBRAS Academy: it shows that
accessible educational technology for deaf learners must be evaluated through what users
actually do with the available resources --- and that evaluating this requires methods
designed for the specific communicational conditions of the population, not only
instruments adapted from hearing-user research.

The methodological contribution is equally concrete. UES-Br produced interpretable
evidence in this study, but only when scores were corrected for negatively worded items,
read alongside observational records, and checked against the conditions under which
each response was produced. Three threats to interpretability --- semantic
inaccessibility of questionnaire vocabulary, social desirability in the researcher
presence, and the relational effect of mediated administration --- define what must be
addressed before engagement can be reliably measured with deaf adolescents in any
evaluation setting. These threats are not specific to this study; they are structural
features of mediated self-report evaluation with this population.

Together, the empirical and methodological contributions of this paper establish a
starting point. The six trajectories described here, the barrier taxonomy derived from
the sessions, and the interpretability threats named from the UES-Br data constitute
grounded, specific evidence that future work can test, extend, and refine. Broader
participation, repeated sessions, and instrument adaptation are the next steps --- not
as standard qualitative caveats, but as research directions that this study defines
precisely enough to act on.
